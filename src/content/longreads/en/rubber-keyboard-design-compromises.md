## A design you feel with your fingers

We have already written separately about the ZX Spectrum’s price: [the machine cost less than its rivals](/en/history/sinclair-price-of-a-television/) because everything unnecessary was deliberately stripped out of it. But thrift has a way of becoming visible — and in this case, tangible to the touch. Its most striking trace is the legendary rubber keyboard, which contemporaries called, without much affection, “dead flesh.”

The thesis of this piece is simple: the Spectrum’s keyboard, case and cooling are not flaws in the design but its logic. Where pricier machines fitted mechanical keys, metal and ventilation, Sinclair removed parts — and it was on these compromises that the affordable price rested. The face (and the fingertips) of these decisions belonged to the industrial designer Rick Dickinson.

## A keyboard “like dead flesh”

The first Spectrum’s keyboard is a single sheet of rubber, moulded as one piece, with 40 keys. Each key was multi-function: Sinclair BASIC keywords and symbols were printed on and around it, so one physical button stood for several functions of the language. This gave compactness — but also that signature feel: the press was soft, without clear tactile feedback, and that is exactly where the “dead flesh” nickname came from.

The criticism was earned: touch-typing on such a keyboard was hard, and frequent presses into the centre of a key gradually wore the shape into the rubber. But the museum should not present this as a mere failure by the designer. The rubber keyboard solved a concrete engineering problem — and solved it efficiently, simply at the cost of comfort.

## Why rubber: the economics beneath the keys

Rick Dickinson joined Sinclair Research in 1979 and became the in-house industrial designer for the whole range, starting with the ZX80. His approach to the keyboard was radical: instead of a conventional mechanism with hundreds of separate parts, he reduced the construction to literally a few moving pieces. The rubber sheet pressed on a membrane beneath it — and that was enough to register a keypress.

The effect is the same one we saw with the ULA chip in the [architecture piece](/en/history/what-is-inside-z80-ula-48k/): every part that is not there means not only money saved on the component but also simpler assembly, fewer points of failure on the line, a thinner case. A mechanical keyboard would have cost a noticeable share of the whole machine’s price — and the £175 Spectrum simply would not have existed with one. Rubber was not a whim but a condition.

> **Design inheritance.** The logic of “remove parts for the sake of price” runs through the whole Spectrum: one ULA chip instead of dozens of ICs, output to a home television instead of a monitor, cassette instead of a disk drive — and a rubber sheet instead of a mechanical keyboard. The keyboard is that same design philosophy, only pushed outward, to where the hand can feel it.

## Heat, case and power

The compromises did not end at the keys. Inside the Spectrum sat a 7805 linear voltage regulator that ran noticeably hot; there was no active cooling, and the heat was carried off only by an internal heatsink. In a compact case without ventilation, this meant the machine worked distinctly warm, and its thermal behaviour was one of the design’s weak points.

The case itself — a black wedge — was also Dickinson’s work, as was the recognisable rainbow stripe beside the keyboard that became the platform’s visual mark. The shape served the same goal: to fit the board, keyboard and connectors into the cheapest and most compact volume possible. The Spectrum’s aesthetic is the aesthetic of thrift, raised to a house style.

## How the keyboard was “fixed”: +, 128 and +2

The market eventually forced Sinclair to soften the compromise. In October 1984 came the ZX Spectrum+ — the same machine inside, but with a mechanical 58-key keyboard, a reset button and a case styled closer to the larger Sinclair QL. The internals stayed the same; what changed was the thing that mattered most to the user — what the fingers touched.

The next step was taken by the brand’s new owner. When the Spectrum passed to Amstrad in 1986, the +2 model gained a full typewriter keyboard in a grey case and a built-in cassette deck. The journey from “dead flesh” to a familiar keyboard took a few years — exactly as long as the market was willing to pay for comfort on top of the base price.

## A compromise that became the platform’s face

The most interesting thing is that it was the “wrong” rubber keyboard that became the symbol of the Spectrum — not the later, more comfortable versions. What began as forced thrift turned into the machine’s recognisable character: the moment you see that rubber sheet with the rainbow stripe, you know at once it is a Spectrum.

For the museum there is a broader lesson here, shared with the whole Spectrum block: the fate of mass-market technology is often decided not in the pursuit of perfection but in the art of a sensible compromise. Dickinson’s keyboard is a textbook example of turning a limitation into a style.

## The museum exhibit

In the museum’s permanent exhibition you can compare both generations directly: the basic 48K model with its rubber keyboard stands beside the ZX Spectrum+, which already has mechanical keys across 58 positions. It is a rare chance to feel the compromise not in a description but literally — to run a finger over the “dead flesh” and over the keys that replaced it.
